The Retzke Surname in Europe: Origins, History & Lineage

26 Nov 2026 · James

Summary

Retzke is a rare surname from the German-Slavic borderlands east of the Elbe, and no authoritative dictionary entry for it exists, so its roots have to be reconstructed from related names and place names. The ending -ke is a Low German diminutive; the stem Retz- most plausibly comes from a shortened Germanic rād ("counsel") name, a Germanized Slavic name, or a village name like Retzow, Retzin or Retztow.

The few verifiable modern bearers point to Brandenburg and Saxony-Anhalt, and the name reached the U.S. Midwest in the 19th-century Pomeranian and Mecklenburg emigration wave. Confidence: meaning is a hypothesis, geography is thin but consistent, and this page marks which claims are sourced and which are inference.

Caution on sources: several name-aggregator sites (for example NameCensus) print confident histories and "famous Retzkes" that could not be verified anywhere else and that contradict each other. They were excluded.

Etymology

The suffix is the solid part: -ke is a diminutive ending of Low German, close in function to High German -chen, and it does not carry a meaning of its own (SGGEE genealogy list). Slavic diminutives in -k- are related to it, which is why many east-German names in -ke are impossible to assign to one language (e-onomastics). The stem Retz- is where the uncertainty sits. Four readings are plausible, built from parallel names in the Dictionary of American Family Names (Hanks, 2nd ed., 2022) as reproduced by Geneanet and 23andMe.

ReadingMechanismParallel namesStrength
Germanic pet formShort form of a rād ("counsel") name such as Konrad, plus -ke: Retz + keRetz (short form of rād-names), Radke, RettkeModerate to high; the most conventional reading
Germanized Slavic namePet form of a Slavic name built on rad ("glad") or rat ("battle"), spelled by German scribesRadtke / Ratke (Sorbian Ratk), Redetzke (Germanized Polish Redecki)Moderate; fits the settlement zone
HabitationalSomeone from a place called Retz-, Retzow, Retzin or RetztowRetz (places in NW Germany and Austria), Reetz (Prignitz villages, probably Slavic), Rettke (also read as from Rettkau near Glogau)Moderate; no bearer tied to a specific village yet
NicknameFrom Middle High German ratzen ("to scratch"), a teaseRetzerLow; the pattern is for -er names, not -ke

The village names are themselves mostly Slavic. Retschow in Mecklenburg is explained as from a Slavic word for rye (GenWiki forum), and Retzow in the Havelland is said to come from a Slavic name meaning lynx (Heraldry Wiki). So even the habitational reading leads back to the Slavic-speaking population that lived between the Elbe and the Oder before German settlement.

Bottom line: the most defensible statement is that Retzke is an east-German name formed with a Low German or Slavic diminutive on a stem Retz-. Choosing between the readings requires a named ancestor and a village, which only church and civil records can supply.

Taken together, the two documented Germans and the Potsdam listing point to Brandenburg and Saxony-Anhalt, which fits a name formed in the Elbe–Oder Slavic contact zone. That is a pattern from three data points, not a proven distribution. The best free check is a name-distribution map: the Verein für Computergenealogie map shows the name for 1890 and for 1996, and Geogen maps it by district (t-online guide). Compare the 1890 map to the modern one to see where the name was before the 1945 expulsions and the postwar moves.

For Europe outside Germany nothing reliable was found. Poland is the likeliest place for older Retzke lines, since much of historic Pomerania, Silesia and the Neumark is now Polish, but no source showing the surname there was found.

History and roots

There is no documented history of a Retzke family as such, so this section describes the world the name most likely grew out of. Background points without a link are general historical knowledge, not findings from this research.

  1. Slavic settlement east of the Elbe (before c. 1100). Slavic-speaking Polabian and related groups lived across what became Mecklenburg, Brandenburg and Pomerania. Village names like Retzow, Retschow and Retzin come from this layer.
  2. German eastern settlement (from the 12th century). Settlers brought Low German into the same villages. Over generations the Slavic speakers shifted to German, but their personal names stayed.
  3. Names written down by German scribes. Surnames from this zone were recorded in Latin letters by Low German scribes, so spellings drifted. A study of Mecklenburg lists about 800 Slavic-origin surnames, and roughly two-thirds were confined to one village or one district (Nordkurier on the Witte study). That local clustering is why a rare name like Retzke can often be traced to a small area.
  4. Fixed surnames (c. 1500–1800). Hereditary family names settled in Brandenburg, Mecklenburg and Pomerania in this period. Church registers, many starting in the 1600s, are the earliest practical source for a Retzke line.
  5. Mass emigration (1870–1890). Nearly two million Germans left, especially from Pomerania and Mecklenburg (Kleier family history). U.S. records for Retzke cluster in Illinois, Wisconsin, Ohio and Michigan (AncientFaces), the same Midwest belt that received those emigrants.
  6. 1945 and after. Pomerania east of the Oder, the Neumark and Silesia passed to Poland, and their German populations were expelled or fled. Places named Retztow and Retzin are now Redostowo and Rzecino in Poland (Ehemalige Ostgebiete, Retzin). A Retzke line from that region may therefore appear in Polish archives under the Polish place name.

The two Retzkes with public biographies are both tied to East Germany (a GDR parliament member and an athlete born in Wolfen), which matches the Brandenburg and Saxony-Anhalt pattern but says nothing about earlier generations.

Global spread

Retzke has a small footprint in two main countries: a third-party estimate counts about 465 bearers worldwide, 234 in Germany and 201 in the United States, with small groups in Brazil and Switzerland (surnam.es). These are estimates from compilations, and they disagree: NameCensus, using the 2020 U.S. Census, reports 129 U.S. bearers (NameCensus). No documented Retzke family that split across several countries was found; the evidence is a surname distribution plus a few individual U.S. records.

United States

  • Where: AncientFaces holds 577 Retzke records, concentrated in Illinois, Wisconsin, Ohio and Michigan, with others in California, Florida, Virginia, Arkansas and Mississippi (AncientFaces).
  • Illinois line: Frank Retzke (1870–1941) and Theresa Soldatke (1875–1970) had a son, also Frank, born in Illinois around 1901 and died in Chicago in 1948 (Ancestry listing). The listing gives no German birthplace. The dates match AncientFaces' entry for a Theresa Retzke (1875–1970), which suggests the same woman.
  • Ohio: a family-tree record places a Paul G. Retzke, born around 1910, in Ohio (MyHeritage).
  • Michigan: a 2026 obituary from Berrien County mentions a Retzke marriage at Immanuel Lutheran Church in Bridgman (Legacy.com).
  • Wisconsin: a 2020 Milwaukee obituary shows a Retzke family in the city (Echovita).

This pattern fits the German settlement belt of the Great Lakes states and the 1870–1890 emigration wave, but none of these records ties a person to a German village, so the link is circumstantial.

Canada

No Retzke was found in Canada. Searches of Canadian family-tree and obituary pages returned only Retzlaff, a different name. A Canadian Retzke is possible, since the surname databases are incomplete, so check the Library and Archives Canada census indexes (1901 to 1921) under Retzke, Rettke and Retske.

Brazil

About 25 bearers are estimated in Brazil. No family records were found, but there is context: Pomeranian immigrants settled in Espírito Santo from the late 1850s, and the municipality of Santa Maria de Jetibá is described as one of the last major East Pomeranian-speaking communities (Wikipedia, UFGD article). A rarer spelling, Rettz, has 7 bearers in Brazil, 71 percent of them in Espírito Santo (Forebears). That makes a Pomeranian route a reasonable hypothesis for Brazilian Retzkes, but it is unverified.

Other regions

  • Switzerland: 5 bearers are estimated; no explanation for them was found.
  • Australia: no Retzke found. A similar Prussian-origin name, Andretzke, appears in South Australia, where one farmer born in Posen emigrated in 1856 (GEDBAS). It shows the same emigrant stream carried -tzke names there, not that Retzke did.
  • Russia: the shorter name Retz appears in a Volga German colony (Volga German Institute). This is a different name, but Retz-type names may have reached eastern Europe too.

To show that U.S. and German Retzkes share an ancestor, you need an emigrant's ship record or naturalization papers naming a home village.

When searching indexes, try tz, z, ts and tsch in the middle, e, ie, ä and ae for the vowel, one or two t, and endings -ke, -ka, -ko in records written by Polish or Latin-language clerks. Indexes made by volunteers often miss these, so browse the original page when a name is near a likely village.

Research guide: tracing a Retzke line

The name can be traced only from a living or recently dead relative backward, so the first step is yours, not the name's.

  1. Start from home sources. Collect full names, birth and marriage places, emigration years and any German place name from relatives, documents, obituaries and gravestones. Write place names as spelled in the document.
  2. Find the U.S. arrival record. Passenger lists, naturalization papers and census entries often state a home village or Prussian province. Hamburg passenger lists (1850–1934) name the last residence of many emigrants.
  3. Pin the village. Look it up in the GOV gazetteer and in Meyers Gazetteer to learn its Kreis, parish and civil registry (Standesamt). Many places now carry Polish names.
  4. Read the church books. Archion holds Protestant books for much of Brandenburg, Mecklenburg and Pomerania (paid access); FamilySearch and Ancestry host transcripts for Mecklenburg and Brandenburg. Civil registers start in 1874 in Prussia.
  5. For lost territory, use Polish state archives (for example Szczecin) and the Pommerscher Greif society for Pomerania.
  6. Check community trees. GEDBAS and Geneanet trees hold user-submitted lines; treat them as leads, since they carry unsourced links.
  7. Test the origin with DNA only as a hint. A commercial "origin" report can place a family in a region, but it cannot confirm a surname's meaning.
